\section{Replication: sequential scorer, intervention-aware baselines, and prospective audits}\label{app:replication-sec}\label{app:replication}
To test the protocol under a nonlinear sequential scorer, selected audit components were replicated with a SASRec-style transformer (item and positional embeddings of dimension $64$, one transformer-encoder layer with a single head and dropout $0.1$, history truncated to the most recent $20$ events, trained by leave-one-out BPR with Adam at learning rate $3\times10^{-4}$ for $10$ epochs; the user representation is the history-weighted mean of encoded positions, recomputed at scoring time so that masking/downweighting acts without retraining; Appendix~\ref{app:replication}). This is an architectural stress test, not evidence from a competitive recommender: neither the cohort recipe nor the longer full-corpus training variant exceeds the popularity reference under full-catalogue ranking (Table~\ref{tab:review5-sasrec-quality}). The replication reports bounded-alignment, exact-estimator, interaction, prospective-audit, and action-selection diagnostics; it does not reproduce the complete primary method-by-metric matrix.

Three patterns emerge (Table~\ref{tab:review3}). First, intervention-aware baselines---bounded binary/continuous LIME, finite differences, and integrated gradients, all trained or integrated along the same weight path used at deployment---reach bounded AIA $0.72$--$1.00$ (bounded binary/continuous LIME $0.73$--$0.95$, finite differences and integrated gradients $0.72$--$1.00$), matching or exceeding binary-mask LIME ($0.74$--$0.91$) and Monte Carlo Shapley ($0.14$--$0.70$): part of binary LIME's weakness is therefore a perturbation-distribution mismatch, and Shapley's coalition averaging does not by itself predict bounded interventions better than path-matched baselines. Second, pair interactions $I_{pq}=\Delta(\{p,q\})-\Delta(\{p\})-\Delta(\{q\})$ are small relative to singleton effects for ItemKNN (mean ratio $0.07$; additive-vs-realized pair ranking $\rho=0.84$--$0.90$), empirically supporting the main manuscript's additive decision heuristic under ItemKNN, but are substantial under SASRec (ratios $0.21$--$0.78$, additive-vs-realized $\rho=0.45$--$0.78$), so interaction-aware selection is recommended whenever the scorer is strongly nonlinear. Third, replacing the held-out target by the model's own top-1 recommendation (a prospective, non-target-conditioned audit) leaves Shapley bounded alignment high (run means $0.73$--$0.87$; $93$--$100\%$ of users attain at least $0.5$ alignment among the $126$--$227$ users per run whose generated top-1 lies in the candidate set), showing that the attribution audit can also be instantiated without future-label conditioning. Finally, a component ablation on the same realized-effect currency shows that the protocol components matter: forcing an action (removing the no-action option) changes the selected action in $82$--$89\%$ of users without improving the mean realized effect (mean $\Delta$ in $[-0.023,+0.001]$, with individual deltas reaching $-0.51$ under SASRec), and magnitude-only selection changes the action in $89$--$96\%$ of users and degrades the mean realized effect under the sequential scorer (mean $\Delta=-0.013$ on Amazon, $-0.041$ on ML-1M), supporting the signed, abstention-aware action-selection rule in the main manuscript.

\subsection{Coalition and scorer-state budgets, and release provenance}
Table~\ref{tab:computational-cost} reports allocation-stage coalition/scorer-state budgets. With $m=|E_u|$ candidates and embedding dimension $d$, each scorer call costs $C_{\mathrm{score}}(m,d)$, giving $O(T\,n_u\,C_{\mathrm{score}})$ for Shapley, $O(S_{\mathrm{LIME}}\,C_{\mathrm{score}}+S_{\mathrm{LIME}}n_u^2+n_u^3)$ including the ridge solve, $O(n_u\,C_{\mathrm{score}})$ for LOO, and $O(n_u^2\,C_{\mathrm{score}})$ for greedy and the exact $B\le2$ oracle. On the replication machine the reverse-paired estimator costs $0.3$--$1.6$\,s per user (ItemKNN/SASRec) versus $0.03$--$0.12$\,s for bounded LIME, confirming that protocol overhead scales with scorer cost as analysed. Peak memory is likewise not serialized for the archived replication runs, and is therefore not claimed for them; the review-$9$ runs do record it (Table~\ref{tab:r9-hardware}). These are analytical scorer-call counts, not claims that every call has identical wall-clock cost; the exact ItemKNN batch implementation reuses cached similarity blocks, while the joint-action oracle is evaluated once per user and shared across methods. Asymptotically in the player count $n_u$, the allocation stage costs $O(T\,n_u)$ cached prefix states for Monte Carlo Shapley with $T=2M_{\mathrm{pair}}$ evaluated orders, $O(S_{\mathrm{LIME}})$ mask evaluations for LIME with $S_{\mathrm{LIME}}=512$, $O(n_u)$ states for leave-one-out, and $O(n_u^2)$ deletion evaluations for greedy sequential deletion; the shared exact $B\le2$ oracle adds $O(n_u^2)$ joint evaluations per user, computed once and reused by every method and utility. All budgets are therefore linear or quadratic in $n_u$ and independent of catalogue size beyond the fixed candidate-set scoring block, which is cached per user.

\subsection*{Extended empirical basis}
Five additional experiment families extend the empirical basis beyond the
primary ItemKNN analysis (Tables~\ref{tab:review8-lightgcn-quality}--\ref{tab:review8-b3}).

\emph{Architecture and dataset generality.} The central finding---that
path-matched baselines (finite differences, integrated gradients, bounded
LIME) exceed Monte Carlo Shapley on bounded-intervention alignment---replicates
under a graph architecture (LightGCN, Table~\ref{tab:review8-generalization})
and on a third dataset from a different domain (Gowalla location check-ins).
LightGCN is adapted to the protocol's inference-time history-weighting
interface; it does not pass the recommendation-quality gate
(Table~\ref{tab:review8-lightgcn-quality}), so it is reported as an
architecture-applicability cell rather than a competitive-recommender cell.
A tuning sweep (deeper propagation, larger embeddings, higher learning rate,
80 epochs, full-corpus training) leaves NDCG@10 below the popularity
reference on both datasets, suggesting that the scoring-time user-vector
interface required for bounded downweighting, rather than the tested training
budget, remains a limiting factor. Obtaining a competitive neural recommender that also
exposes the bounded history-weighting interface therefore remains an open
engineering requirement.

\emph{Monte Carlo error control at the cap.} The earlier exact-validation gap---
convergence was only validated exactly for $n_u\le12$ while $83.1\%$ of
MovieLens users sit at the $n_{\max}=20$ cap---is addressed by per-user Monte Carlo
standard errors plus an independent larger-budget reference
(Table~\ref{tab:review8-mcse}): relative standard errors are small (mean
$0.06$--$0.08$ of the largest $|\phi|$) and the estimate at the diagnostic budget
$M_{\mathrm{pair}}=250$ agrees with the independent $M_{\mathrm{pair}}=1000$ reference
at rank correlation $\ge0.948$ for every one of the 50 sampled cap users per
dataset (mean $0.989$ MovieLens, $0.997$ Amazon).

\emph{Exhaustive budget-3 oracle.} The greedy budget-3 lower bound is replaced
by exhaustive enumeration of all $\le3$ actions ($1{,}351$ at $n=20$,
Table~\ref{tab:review8-b3}); additive selection attains low regret at both
$B=2$ and $B=3$.

\emph{Worked example.} A representative MovieLens user (target item $1344$,
$n_u=20$) illustrates the knowledge-level output: the two most-beneficial
interactions by attribution (items $336$ and $2094$, both negative $\phi$) are
selected by the additive rule, and the resulting joint action coincides exactly
with the exact budget-2 oracle (realized target-margin effect $+0.0031$).

\section{Review-9 inference audits and construct-validity ablations}\label{app:review9}
The tables in this section and Table~\ref{tab:S4} are generated from the frozen release matrices and are typeset against result manifest \texttt{\resultmanifeststamp}, the same manifest quoted in the main paper.
This section collects the analyses added in the ninth revision round. All of them
are computed from the frozen release matrices by
\texttt{scripts/make\_review9\_stats.py}, so they are reproducible from the
artifact without re-running any recommender, and the generated file is written
byte-identically to both table mirrors.

\emph{Populations (Issue 10).} Table~\ref{tab:r9-inclusion-flow} gives the exact
denominator of every inferential statement in the paper, and
Table~\ref{tab:r9-all-user-sensitivity} repeats each population-conditional
statistic over all users with finite values under both methods, which is the
all-user sensitivity the review asked for alongside the inclusion flow.

\emph{Multiplicity (Issue 18).} Table~\ref{tab:r9-multiplicity-map} lists family
membership, family size, the raw exceedance count and the Holm multiplier for the
headline contrasts; recomputing all $2{,}312$ released tests from their raw
$p$-values reproduces every published adjusted $p$-value, and Table~\ref{tab:r9-permutation-precision} shows that residual differences between
pipelines are Monte Carlo error of the declared $10{,}000$-draw experiment.
Table~\ref{tab:S4} is now generated from the same released per-test file as the
main paper, with the exceedance count and the authoritative 12-contrast adjusted
$p$ printed next to each other; this replaced eight legacy values that had been
produced with a smaller permutation budget, including the MovieLens
Shapley--LIME success contrast, which was printed as $.0066$ and is $.0048$ with
$15$ exceedances of $10{,}000$ (the $.0216$ in the predeclared family is the
same raw evidence corrected with a $\times9$ rather than $\times3$ multiplier).

\emph{Utility mismatch (Issue 4).} Table~\ref{tab:r9-utility-factorial} reports
the attribution-utility $\times$ outcome-utility factorial on the primary cohort,
which separates utility mismatch from interaction effects in the H2 adjudication.
Table~\ref{tab:r9-utility-factorial-replication} repeats the design on the benchmark
that ships inside the artifact (Gowalla, Monte Carlo Shapley, the same
$M_{\mathrm{pair}}=250$ budget and $\rho$ as the ablation below): holding the
attribution fixed and changing only the utility the action is evaluated under moves
the mean bounded AIA by $+0.330$ ($95\%$ CI $[0.248,0.415]$), and the mirrored cell
moves by $-0.275$ ($[-0.438,-0.101]$). A single utility substitution is thus
comparable in size to the primary cohort's own method contrasts (Shapley--LIME
bounded AIA differs by $0.155$ on MovieLens and $0.413$ on Amazon), which is exactly
the confound the review raised---and it is why H2 is adjudicated on rank association
rather than on the level of the alignment score. The NDCG-conditioned cells contain
only $14$ of $250$ sampled users ($11$ with an active oracle), so those cells are
reported as descriptive counts and no inference is drawn from them here; a powered
version of the full $2\times2\times2$ design needs the primary cohorts re-scored at
the larger candidate budget.

\emph{Estimator uncertainty (Issue 9).}
Table~\ref{tab:r9-mc-propagation} propagates per-user Monte Carlo error into the
reported alignment statistic instead of only reporting agreement diagnostics.

\emph{Randomization-test validity (Issue 15).}
Table~\ref{tab:r9-studentized} repeats every headline paired contrast under both
the published plus-one sign-flip test and a studentized bootstrap-$t$ test that
needs no symmetry of the paired differences. Skew is not innocuous for these
statistics (normalized regret $4.17$, target-margin effect $-5.68$ on Amazon), but
no significance decision changes: the nine sign-flip tests at the
$2{\times}10^{-5}$ floor all remain below $0.001$ when studentized, the three
non-significant Amazon contrasts stay non-significant, and the largest disagreement
between the two procedures is the MovieLens realized-NDCG contrast, where the
sign-flip $p$ of $0.0045$ becomes $0.0012$---a factor of $3.8$ in a regime where
neither value is near the decision boundary.

\emph{Stratified within-user nulls (Issue 12).} An unstratified within-user null
treats every reordering of a profile as equally implausible, so
Table~\ref{tab:r9-stratified-null} re-derives it under two structure-preserving
redraws on the replication benchmark. The reviewer's worry is real in degree: the
null mean moves from $-0.0001$ unstratified to $0.100$ under recency blocks and
$0.192$ under popularity blocks, so part of the raw alignment is explained by the
profile's own temporal and popularity structure and an unstratified test understates
the baseline. The observed bounded AIA of $0.575$ nonetheless sits $27.1$, $22.9$
and $19.1$ null standard deviations above the three nulls, and no redraw in a
thousand reaches as high as $0.062$, $0.174$ and $0.252$ respectively. The released
null uses $1{,}000$ redraws, so its plus-one $p$ saturates at $0.0010$ in every row
and the standardized distance is the comparable statistic; the primary-cohort tests
use $10{,}000$ draws against the unstratified null
(Table~\ref{tab:r9-multiplicity-map}), and the stratified version is reported here on
the benchmark whose per-user intervention files ship inside the artifact.

\emph{Intervention semantics (Issue 1).} Dividing by the retained weight sum makes
the deletion intervention a reallocation of profile mass rather than isolated
suppression, so the review asked whether the bounded effect is an artifact of that
normalization. Tables~\ref{tab:r9-fixed-denominator}
and~\ref{tab:r9-fixed-denominator-paired} answer it by re-scoring the same fitted
neighbourhoods under both denominators: nothing is retrained and the candidate sets,
permutation seed, intervention strength ($\rho=0.5$) and evaluated users are
identical, so any difference is the interface itself. The ablation is run on
Gowalla, the third benchmark that ships in the artifact ($600$ sampled users,
$528$ of which have a defined $n_{\max}=20$ attribution game); the primary cohorts
require per-user intervention files that are not part of the frozen matrices.

The concern is confirmed on this benchmark. Under the normalized interface the
Shapley bounded AIA is $0.638$ and the leave-one-out bounded AIA is $0.990$, so
leave-one-out looks like the most aligned scorer; under a fixed denominator the
two are indistinguishable ($0.998$ versus $1.000$) and ItemKNN-LOO and Shapley
swap places ahead of LIME ($0.690$ normalized, $0.700$ fixed). The paired user
contrast on Shapley is $-0.360$ ($95\%$ CI $[-0.375,-0.346]$, $d_z=-2.07$, all
$528$ users nonzero), and the signed-alignment version of the same statistic moves
by $-0.298$ ($d_z=-1.87$). The bounded-minus-deletion gap, which is the quantity
the objection targets, is $+0.0098$ under normalization and $0.0000$ under pure
suppression: the normalization does inflate the gap, but on this cohort the gap is
small in absolute terms either way and is carried by $29$ of $528$ users, which is
why its floor $p$-value is marked as zero-inflated rather than read as a strong
effect. The decisive point is the joint reading of level and scale: the realized
mean absolute singleton effect is $3.2\times10^{-4}$ (normalized) versus
$2.3\times10^{-4}$ (fixed), so a bounded AIA of $0.998$ on this benchmark is
saturation of a ratio whose numerator and denominator shrink together, not
evidence of a strong suppression effect. Bounded AIA is therefore interpreted in
this paper only as a comparison within one interface---the exact inference-time
rule of the fitted scorer---and never as an absolute amount of evidence; the
tables above give the pure-suppression counterfactual for every claim that could be
read the second way.
\emph{Target conditioning (Issue 5).} Conditioning the attribution game on the held-out
item makes part of ``alignment'' definitional, so the audit was re-run with the target
replaced by the scorer's own top-$1$ prospective recommendation, on the benchmark that
ships inside the artifact and with everything else held fixed ($600$ sampled users,
$M_{\mathrm{pair}}=250$, $n_{\max}=20$, evaluation set $200$, $\rho=0.5$, seed $42$,
normalized interface). Table~\ref{tab:r9-prospective-replication} reports the
$528$ users whose prospective game has a defined alignment score: Shapley bounded AIA
$0.676$ (SD $0.176$, users ranging from $-0.456$ to $0.976$), bounded LIME $0.697$,
leave-one-out $0.9997$, signed alignment $0.701$. The same cohort under
held-out conditioning gives $0.638$, $0.690$, $0.990$ and $0.700$
(Table~\ref{tab:r9-fixed-denominator}), so removing the future label moves the two
attribution-based scores by $+0.038$ and $+0.008$ and leaves the cross-scorer ordering
untouched: the level is not an artifact of conditioning on the target, and
leave-one-out remains saturated whichever target the game is built around. Only
$72$ of $600$ audited users ($12.0\%$) have a prospective top-$1$ that coincides with
the held-out item, so the two audits mostly score different events and a per-user
paired contrast between them is not a defined quantity; what is comparable is the
ordering and the size of the alignment, and both survive. The residual caveat is
coverage, not conditioning: for the remaining users the audit measures alignment to a
target nobody consumed, which is the deployment-relevant estimand but not the one the
primary analysis reports.

\emph{Measured cost (Issue 13).} Scorer-call budgets do not by themselves price a
protocol, so the review-$9$ runner serializes its environment and times each
attribution method on the same fitted neighbourhoods
(Table~\ref{tab:r9-hardware}): x86-64 Linux on $2$ cores, Python $3.11.2$, NumPy
$2.4.6$, peak resident set of the process $305$\,MB, and $5$ repeats over $40$ users
yielding medians of $2.11$\,s per user for reverse-paired Monte Carlo Shapley at
$M_{\mathrm{pair}}=250$, $0.149$\,s for bounded LIME at $512$ masks and $0.0059$\,s for
leave-one-out, i.e. $14\times$ and $360\times$ the LIME and LOO costs respectively at
the published budgets. Equalizing the budget rather than reporting it is the harder
half of the objection, and the tooling for it is in the artifact:
\texttt{compute-matched} gives Shapley and LIME the same $2M_{\mathrm{pair}}(n_u{+}1)$
scorer evaluations at every point of a grid and typesets the matched-budget table
as soon as a cohort has been run; on the machine above one grid point on the
$12$-user pilot cohort costs $47$\,s, so a full curve is a batch job and no
matched-budget alignment claim is made in this document.
